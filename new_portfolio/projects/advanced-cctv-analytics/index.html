    CCTV video analytics in Python: crowds, crime and faces

    Three watchers for camera footage.

    CCTV video analytics in Python: this Smart India Hackathon team prototype watches camera footage three ways. A crowd counter raises a limit alert, a crime module labels sampled video frames and a face module recognises enrolled people. The crowd and crime modules save evidence frames to MongoDB and send WhatsApp alerts. Co-built with Prasanna Patil.

    Overview

    Why this matters

    Shops, event venues, campuses and transport hubs often run more cameras than their security staff can watch at once. Crowds build past a safe size, fights start and people who should not be on site walk in, often while a screen goes unwatched. Software that watches the footage, sends a short alert and keeps the picture behind it is meant to help a small team spot these moments sooner.

    A good result is an alert that a person can trust and check. It says what was seen, keeps the frame behind it and does not flood the team with false alarms. For a buyer, that means measuring each alert type on their own footage before relying on it, and setting privacy rules before any face data is stored.

    The story

    The problem: too much footage, too few eyes

    The problem

    Public-safety teams cannot watch every camera at once. Overcrowding, violent incidents and known persons of interest slip past exactly when attention matters most. The cost is a slower response, more risk to the people on site and more damage or liability for the venue, because a team that checks screens by eye can only react after someone happens to notice.

    What I built

    The prototype splits monitoring into three separate modules. The crowd module removes the video background to find moving shapes, tracks each shape and counts people crossing two virtual lines, then raises an alert at a set limit. The crime module checks one frame every 3 seconds with a pre-trained Vision Transformer (ViT, an image-classification model) that returns one of 14 labels. The face module finds faces with Haar cascades (a classic face finder) and names them with an LBPH matcher (a classic face-matching method), after a small tool enrols the people to look for. The crowd and crime modules save evidence frames to MongoDB and send alerts through Twilio WhatsApp. Flask pages show the latest saved frame.

    What made it hard

    1. 01

      Overlapping people in a crowd

      People who walk close together merge into one moving shape. The sample crowd clip in the repository is a dense street scene, so counting by shapes is rough and every count is an estimate.

    2. 02

      Judging crime from a still frame

      The classifier sees one picture every 3 seconds, with no sense of movement, so the context that makes an event a crime is often missing. Its labels are hints for a person to check.

    3. 03

      Alerts that come with evidence

      A bare alarm is hard to trust or act on. Each alert needs a saved frame and, for crime, a label to check. That means storing images and reading them back for review.

    4. 04

      Faces are personal data

      Recognising a person means keeping their face photos. That raises consent, retention and access questions that this prototype does not yet handle.

    How it is built

    Crowd alerts, crime labels and face matching

    1. 01Video inFile or webcam footage
    2. 02AnalyseCrowd, crime or face; face ends here
    3. 03DecideLimit reached or label found
    4. 04SaveCrowd and crime frames to MongoDB
    5. 05AlertCrowd and crime texts via Twilio
    6. 06ReviewFlask page shows latest frame
    1. 01

      Crowd module: OpenCV background subtraction (MOG2) finds moving shapes, and a simple tracker gives each one an ID. Two virtual lines count people crossing in each direction. At the limit of 12 it saves the frame, beeps twice, sends a WhatsApp alert and starts the review page server.

    2. 02

      Crime module: a pre-trained Vision Transformer, published on the Hugging Face model hub as UCF_Crime, labels one frame every 3 seconds with one of 14 UCF-Crime labels: 13 incident types, such as Fighting or Robbery, and a normal label.

    3. 03

      Face module: Haar cascades locate faces in a webcam image. A small tkinter window enrols a person by saving cropped 200 by 200 pixel photos, and an LBPH matcher then shows a name, or "not recognized", on screen.

    4. 04

      Evidence store: the crowd and crime modules save each evidence frame as an image in MongoDB, a database. The crime module keeps the label with the frame and skips a frame whose exact pixels were already stored.

    5. 05

      Review pages: two small Flask pages show the latest saved frame from MongoDB, one for crowd alerts and one for crime labels, where the label sits above the picture.

    6. 06

      Alerts: the Twilio WhatsApp API sends a short text when the crowd limit is reached, carrying the ID of the saved frame, and one text when the crime module finishes a video. Neither message includes the picture.

    7. 07

      Inputs and settings: the crowd and crime modules read a video file chosen in a file dialog, and the face tool reads a webcam. The crowd limit and the crime sampling interval are plain variables in the scripts.

    Decisions

    Design decisions and trade-offs

    • Three separate modules, not one pipeline

      ChoiceCrowd counting, crime labelling and face recognition are separate programs, each with its own input and settings. The crowd and crime programs each have their own review page.

      WhyEach can be run, tested and replaced on its own, and a fault in one does not stop the others. The cost is that they do not yet share one feed or one dashboard.

    • A published crime model, not a new one

      ChoiceThe crime module loads a Vision Transformer published on the Hugging Face model hub as UCF_Crime, with the 14 UCF-Crime labels. The repository holds no training code.

      WhyIt gives usable labels without collecting and labelling footage first. The trade-off is that its accuracy on a buyer's own cameras is unknown until it is measured.

    • Classic OpenCV methods for people and faces

      ChoiceThe prototype finds people by background subtraction and counting lines, from a public example, and faces by Haar cascades with an LBPH matcher, rather than a trained detector.

      WhyThese methods are light, need no training data for counting and only a few dozen photos per person for faces. The trade-off is weaker results in dense crowds, poor light and side-on faces.

    • Evidence frames stored in MongoDB

      ChoiceEach alert keeps its frame as an image in MongoDB, and a Flask page reads back the latest one for a reviewer.

      WhyA reviewer sees what the system saw, not only a text alert. The trade-off is growing storage and the need for access and retention rules for stored images.

    Results

    What the prototype shows and does not show

    Every number comes with how it was measured. The limits are part of the result.

    Results

    • Three separate monitoring modules: a crowd counter, a crime labeller and a face recogniser.
    • The crime model has 14 labels, 13 incident types and a normal label, and the code checks one frame every 3 seconds. Both are settings read from the code, not measured accuracy.
    • On the sample crowd clip in the repository, the counter reached its limit of 12 crossings. The saved frame and a count log with 12 entries are kept with the code.
    • The crowd and crime modules save evidence frames and labels to MongoDB, with Flask pages that show the latest saved frame.
    • The team won the college's internal round of the Smart India Hackathon.

    Limits to keep in mind

    • No accuracy was measured. Counts, crime labels and face matches were not tested against labelled footage, so the prototype shows the idea and the working parts, not a reliability figure.
    • The crowd limit counts people crossing downward, not people present at once. Line positions are fixed numbers, overlapping shapes are likely to be counted as one, and the program stops after its first alert.
    • The crime module takes the top label for each sampled frame, with no confidence cut-off, on a 3-second timer that follows the computer clock, not the video. Its one WhatsApp text goes out when the video ends, whatever was found.
    • It is a set of desktop scripts for one computer, reading video files or a local webcam, not network camera streams, with no automated tests. Keys, phone numbers and paths sit inside the code, and the keys are blank in the public copy.

    FAQ

    Questions about the CCTV prototype

    What is video analytics in CCTV?

    Video analytics is software that watches camera footage and reports what it sees, such as how many people are in a place, unusual events or known faces. This prototype has three separate modules: crowd counting with an alert, crime labels from sampled frames and face recognition. The crowd and crime modules save their evidence to a database.

    How accurate is the crime classifier?

    I do not quote an accuracy figure, because I did not measure one on labelled footage. The module uses a pre-trained Vision Transformer with 14 UCF-Crime labels and checks one frame every 3 seconds. A client project would start by measuring it on footage from your own site, then tune the alert rules.

    Did you build all of it yourself?

    No. It was a team project, co-built with Prasanna Patil for the Smart India Hackathon. I was a member of the team and I do not claim sole credit for any module. The code was uploaded in one batch from his account, so its history cannot show who wrote what. The team won the college's internal round.

    How does the crowd counter decide when to raise an alert?

    It removes the video background to find moving shapes, tracks each one and counts people who cross a virtual line. When the count reaches the limit, set to 12 in the code, it saves the frame to MongoDB, beeps, sends a WhatsApp text and stops. The limit counts crossings downward, not people present at once.

    How does the face recognition part work?

    A small window enrols a person by saving cropped photos from a webcam. OpenCV's LBPH recogniser, a classic face-matching method, is then trained on those photos. Haar cascades find faces in the live image and the screen shows a name or "not recognized". It follows public tutorials, sends no alerts and keeps no evidence frames, only the enrolment photos.

    Which crimes can the model label?

    The model has 14 labels: Abuse, Arrest, Arson, Assault, Burglary, Explosion, Fighting, Road accidents, Robbery, Shooting, Shoplifting, Stealing, Vandalism and a normal-video label. It picks one label for each sampled frame, with no confidence cut-off, so a label is a hint for a person to check and not a finding.

    Is this prototype ready for a real control room?

    No. It is a hackathon prototype made of desktop scripts that run on one computer and read video files or a webcam. Settings sit inside the code and there are no automated tests. A real deployment would need network camera input, accuracy tests on your footage, alert rules, privacy controls and monitoring.
