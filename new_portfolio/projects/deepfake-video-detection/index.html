    Deepfake video detection project

    Frame-by-frame verdicts with a downloadable report.

    This deepfake video detection project is a college team project. A small neural network scores every frame of an uploaded video as real or fake. A Streamlit web app shows a confidence chart and exports a PDF report. It reached 74.16% test accuracy on a random frame split. Co-built with Prasanna Patil and a classmate.

    Overview

    Why this matters

    Newsrooms, banks that check identity on video, hiring teams and online platforms may receive video that has been altered. A face-swap clip can mislead an audience, pass a video identity check or damage a person's name. Reviewers who check every clip by eye cannot keep up, so they need a fast first filter that shows what to inspect.

    A useful result is a first check, not a verdict. It scores a clip, shows which frames raised the score and produces a report a reviewer can file with the case. It also states how it was tested and where it fails, so that nobody mistakes a screening tool for proof.

    The story

    The problem: telling real video from fake

    The problem

    Face-swap videos are hard to spot by eye, so reviewers need a quick, explainable first check. Without one, every clip needs careful manual review, and a fake that slips through can reach the news, a customer identity check or a hiring process. A bare yes or no also cannot be explained to a manager or a customer.

    What I built

    The team trained the model on Celeb-DF, a public set of real and face-swapped videos. The first ten frames of each video are resized to 224 by 224 pixels and varied slightly with small shifts, zooms and flips. A small three-block neural network (a CNN, a model type for images) learns from the training split of 7,856 frames to give each frame a real-or-fake score. A six-page Streamlit app lets a reviewer upload a video. It scores every frame, averages the scores into a Real or Fake verdict, charts the score per frame and explains the score bands. A PDF report can be downloaded.

    What made it hard

    1. 01

      Faint traces of a face swap

      A good swap leaves only faint traces, such as small texture and colour mismatches around the face. A small model has to learn to spot them in single frames.

    2. 02

      Near-identical frames

      The first ten frames of a video look almost the same. A random split can put near copies in both training and test data, which can flatter the accuracy.

    3. 03

      Limited computing resources

      The team's report says limited computing resources restrict training depth. The model is small and trains for 5 epochs, and the app scores each frame of an upload one at a time.

    4. 04

      Explaining the verdict

      A reviewer needs more than the word "Fake". The app shows a score for every frame, a chart over the clip and a plain reading of the score bands, and the PDF keeps a record.

    How it is built

    How each frame gets a verdict

    1. 01UploadVideo added in the Streamlit app
    2. 02FramesEvery frame resized to 224 by 224
    3. 03ScoreCNN gives each frame a fake score
    4. 04AverageMean above 50 percent means Fake
    5. 05ShowVerdict, band message and frame chart
    6. 06ReportPDF with verdict and frame scores
    1. 01

      Data: Celeb-DF videos from the Celeb-real and YouTube-real folders are labelled real, those from Celeb-synthesis fake. The first ten frames of each video are resized to 224 by 224 pixels and split 80 to 20 at random, giving 7,856 training frames in the notebook's latest run.

    2. 02

      Augmentation: training frames are varied slightly with rotation up to 10 degrees, small shifts, shear, zoom and horizontal flips. Test frames are only scaled to a 0 to 1 range, so the test is not changed by augmentation.

    3. 03

      Model: a small three-block CNN. Three convolution layers of 32, 64 and 128 filters, each followed by pooling, then a dense layer of 64 units and a single 0 to 1 output (a sigmoid). It has about 5.6 million parameters and trains for 5 epochs with the Adam optimiser.

    4. 04

      Verdict: each frame gets a score from 0 to 1. The app's confidence score is the mean of all frame scores as a percentage, and above 50 is Fake. It reads the bands as above 80 high confidence of a deepfake, 50 to 80 moderate, below 50 appears genuine.

    5. 05

      App: a six-page Streamlit app with Home, Upload Video, Detection Results, How It Works, Feedback and About pages. It accepts mp4, mov, avi and mpeg files, previews the upload, scores every frame and draws a frame-by-frame chart of the scores.

    6. 06

      Report: ReportLab builds a PDF with the video name, verdict, score, a reading guide for the bands and the first 40 frame scores.

    7. 07

      Model delivery: the saved model file is fetched from Google Drive with gdown and cached by Streamlit, because the README treats a file over 25 MB as too big to upload.

    Decisions

    Design decisions and trade-offs

    • A small CNN on single frames

      ChoiceThe team trained a small three-block CNN on single frames, which its report records as a 3-layer ConvNet with augmentation and a sigmoid output.

      WhyA small model trains with limited computing resources, which the report cites as a constraint, and it is simple to explain. The result is modest accuracy: 74.16%, which the report calls room for improvement.

    • Score every frame, then average

      ChoiceThe app scores every frame of an upload, averages the scores into one verdict and plots the score of each frame.

      WhyThe chart shows which parts of a clip drove the verdict, so a reviewer can check those frames by eye. The average keeps the final answer simple.

    • Plain bands for the score

      ChoiceThe app turns the average into three messages: above 80 high confidence of a deepfake, 50 to 80 moderate, below 50 appears genuine. The PDF repeats this guide.

      WhyA reviewer who is not a data scientist can act on a band. The cut-offs are fixed numbers, not calibrated probabilities, so they guide attention and prove nothing.

    • Celeb-DF and a random frame split

      ChoiceTraining used Celeb-DF, which the report calls a clean dataset, with a random 80 to 20 split of frames and a fixed seed so runs can be repeated.

      WhyOne public dataset and a fixed seed keep the work repeatable. The trade-off is optimistic testing, because frames from one video can land on both sides, which a split by video would avoid.

    Results

    Accuracy and test notes

    Every number comes with how it was measured. The limits are part of the result.

    Results

    • 74.16% test accuracy on a random 20% share of frames held out from training, measured by the notebook's evaluate step. The team's own report lists this as room for improvement.
    • 7,856 training frames of 224 by 224 pixels, from the first ten frames of each video in the Celeb-DF folders used. The saved model has about 5.6 million parameters, counted from the model file.
    • An end-to-end flow from upload to scoring, chart and PDF report in the app code, with screenshots of the upload, results and chart pages in the team's report.
    • The PDF report lists the verdict, the score, a reading guide for the bands and the first 40 frame scores.
    • A 31-page project report with requirements, a risk table, a project plan and a literature survey of four papers.

    Limits to keep in mind

    • The 74.16% comes from a random split of frames, so frames of one video can sit in both training and test data. It likely flatters results on new videos, and the notebook gives no split of missed fakes and false alarms.
    • The model looks at whole frames, not cropped faces, and learned from the first ten frames of Celeb-DF videos only. The team's report says it is limited to videos like Celeb-DF and does not cover audio fakes.
    • The notebook's saved outputs come from different runs. The accuracy printout and the cell that shows 7,856 training frames were not run together, so treat them as the team's reported numbers, not one clean experiment.
    • The app saves each upload to a temporary file and does not remove it, and its score is an average of frame scores, not a calibrated probability. Treat the result as a first check for a reviewer, not as proof.

    FAQ

    Questions about deepfake detection

    What is deepfake detection?

    Deepfake detection is software that checks whether a video has been faked, for example by swapping one person's face onto another. This project scores each frame as real or fake and combines the scores into a verdict. It is a first check for reviewers, not proof, because its accuracy is 74.16%.

    How can you detect a deepfake video?

    Use software as a first check and a person for the final call. This project resizes frames and scores each one with a small neural network. It averages the scores into Real or Fake and shows a confidence chart per frame. With 74.16% accuracy it points a reviewer where to look, and it cannot settle the question.

    How accurate is the detector?

    The notebook reports 74.16% on a random 20% share of Celeb-DF frames held out from training, so it is right about three times in four. Frames of one video can sit on both sides of the split, so results on new videos may be lower. The team's report lists it as room for improvement.

    Who worked on it?

    A three-person college team: Prasanna Patil, a classmate and me. The repository is published from my account. The team's report does not record who did which part, so I describe it as shared work rather than claiming a particular module as mine.

    Does the detector find and crop faces first?

    No. The model sees the whole frame, resized to 224 by 224 pixels, with no face detection or cropping in the code. The README describes a ResNeXt and LSTM design (an image network plus a sequence model) and an app diagram shows face cropping, but the code builds neither. A face detector is an extension I can add.

    What data was the model trained on?

    Celeb-DF, a public deepfake dataset. Videos in its Celeb-real and YouTube-real folders are labelled real, and the Celeb-synthesis folder is labelled fake. The first ten frames of each video were resized to 224 by 224 pixels and split 80 to 20 at random, which gave 7,856 training frames. Training added small shifts, zooms and flips.

    Can I use this as proof that a video is fake?

    No. It is a first-check tool with 74.16% accuracy on one dataset, and its score is an average of frame scores, not a calibrated probability. The team's report says the tool is meant for education and not for public accusation. Use it to decide which clips a person should examine closely.
