    Speech emotion recognition project

    Hear the mood in a voice clip, live from the mic.

    This speech emotion recognition project is a neural network (a 1-D CNN, which reads audio as a sequence) that labels seven emotions in speech clips. It was trained on four public datasets and scored 93.87% on a random 20% test split. It comes with live microphone input and a web dashboard.

    Overview

    Why this matters

    Teams that record customer calls or voice messages cannot listen to every recording. Mood can show in the voice as well as in the words, so a caller who sounds angry or frightened can be missed by a search of the transcript. A model that labels emotion from audio could help a team decide which recordings deserve a person's time.

    A good result is a model that labels the emotion in a short clip, shows how sure it is, and comes with a score that says how it was measured and what it does not prove. This project is that first step, on public recordings of speakers. Using it on real calls would need a fresh test on that team's own audio.

    The story

    The problem: finding the mood in a voice

    The problem

    Emotion lives in tone, pitch and energy, not in the words, so a text-based model is the wrong tool. A transcript of a call keeps the words and loses the tone, so an angry caller and a calm one can read the same. A team that works from text alone can miss the very signal that shows which customers need a person.

    What I built

    I started from a public training recipe on Kaggle and adapted it. The four datasets are merged into 12,162 labelled clips, and each clip becomes four versions: the original plus three altered copies, giving 48,648 examples. librosa turns each example into 2,376 numbers that describe the sound of the voice. A five-block neural network then learns seven emotions from those numbers. I tested it on a random 20% of the examples and serve it through notebooks, including live microphone input, and a Streamlit dashboard.

    What made it hard

    1. 01

      Four datasets, one label set

      The four public datasets cover emotions differently: CREMA-D has no surprise clips and RAVDESS has a separate calm label. They had to be merged into one set of seven labels, with calm counted as neutral.

    2. 02

      Uneven emotion examples

      Surprise has 652 clips, against 1,923 for each of angry, disgust, fear, happy and sad. The model sees far fewer examples of it, and the test set holds only 505 surprise samples.

    3. 03

      Long training time

      The network has 7.19 million parameters. Its 20 training passes took about ten hours in total, roughly half an hour each, so every retrain is a planned run, not a quick test.

    4. 04

      A score that can flatter

      Altered copies of one clip can sit on both sides of the split, the same speakers appear on both sides, and the test split doubled as the validation set. The score can therefore overstate how well it handles new voices.

    My role

    My role in the build

    My part

    • Testing the retrained model on a random 20% split, reading the per-emotion scores and stating the limits of that test plainly.
    • The inference notebook: it loads the saved model, predicts from an audio file and listens to the microphone in a live loop.

    Context and credits

    • The training recipe (data merge, audio altering, features and the 1-D network) comes from a public Kaggle notebook. I adapted and retrained it. The model design is not my own invention.
    • The four datasets are public collections of speakers reading fixed sentences or words in each emotion. I recorded no audio, and no real calls or customers were involved.
    • The dashboard is a local prototype. The public repository holds the notebooks, the model design and the preprocessing files, not the dashboard or the large trained weights.

    How it is built

    Four public datasets, seven emotions

    1. 01Voice clipFrom file or microphone
    2. 02Audio features2,376 numbers per clip
    3. 03ScalingSame scale as training
    4. 04Neural networkFive convolution blocks
    5. 05Emotion labelOne of seven emotions
    6. 06Notebook or dashboardLabel; dashboard adds probabilities
    1. 01

      Four public datasets (RAVDESS, CREMA-D, TESS and SAVEE) merged into 12,162 clips, with RAVDESS calm recordings counted as neutral, so every clip has one of seven emotion labels.

    2. 02

      Each clip is read as a 2.5-second window and made into four versions: the original, one with added noise, one pitch-shifted and one pitch-shifted with noise. That gives 48,648 samples.

    3. 03

      librosa audio features: zero-crossing rate, energy and MFCCs (numbers that describe the sound of a voice), 2,376 numbers per sample, put on one scale with a scaler fitted on the training data only.

    4. 04

      A five-block 1-D neural network (convolution, normalisation and pooling) with steps to prevent overfitting (memorising the training clips), then a 512-unit layer and a seven-way output. It has 7.19 million parameters.

    5. 05

      Training uses the Adam optimiser for 20 passes over a random 80% of the samples (38,918), in batches of 64. The other 20% (9,730) is the test set.

    6. 06

      A notebook for live microphone input: it records 2.5 seconds at a time, extracts the same features, scales them and prints the predicted emotion, then repeats until stopped.

    7. 07

      A Streamlit dashboard: upload a WAV file or record, then see the emotion, its confidence, class probabilities and waveform and spectrogram views.

    Decisions

    Design decisions and trade-offs

    • Adapt a public recipe

      ChoiceI chose to adapt a public Kaggle training recipe over designing a network from scratch.

      WhyThe recipe already covered the data merge, audio altering and feature steps. My effort went into retraining it, testing it honestly and making it usable live from a microphone.

    • Merge four datasets

      ChoiceI trained on four merged datasets (RAVDESS, CREMA-D, TESS and SAVEE) rather than on one.

      WhyTogether they hold 12,162 clips from 121 speakers, by the datasets' own descriptions. One dataset alone can have as few as two speakers and one recording style.

    • Keep four versions of each clip

      ChoiceI kept the recipe's four versions of each clip (original, noise, pitch shift, pitch shift with noise) rather than training on the original clips alone.

      WhyMore varied examples make it harder for the network to lean on one recording's quirks. The cost is that copies of one clip can fall on both sides of the split, so the score does not prove new-voice accuracy.

    • Report the final model

      ChoiceI report the score of the final saved model (93.87%) rather than the best single training pass (95.24%).

      WhyBoth numbers come from the same test split, and choosing the best pass by its test score would flatter the model. The final model is the one that is saved and used.

    Results

    Results and a test note

    Every number comes with how it was measured. The limits are part of the result.

    Results

    • Seven emotions are recognised: angry, disgust, fear, happy, neutral, sad and surprise.
    • 93.87% accuracy and a weighted F1 score of 0.94 (one number that balances missed cases against false alarms) on a random 20% test split of 9,730 samples.
    • Per-emotion F1 scores run from 0.92 (sad) to 0.97 (surprise), measured on the same test split.
    • The score is for the final model, the one that is saved and used. An earlier training pass reached 95.24% on the same split; I quote the final figure because picking the best pass would flatter the model.
    • Caution: the test split can hold altered copies of clips that were also used in training, so the score does not show how it performs on new speakers.
    • Live microphone detection runs in a notebook and in the Streamlit dashboard. It has not been scored, so no accuracy figure is claimed for live speech.

    Limits to keep in mind

    • The test is not fully independent. Altered copies of one clip, and the same speakers, appear on both sides of the split, and the test split doubled as the validation set during training. Accuracy on new voices is likely to be lower.
    • Every clip is a speaker reading fixed words or sentences in a requested emotion. Real calls bring background noise, phone-quality audio, other languages and mixed feelings, none of which this model has been tested on.
    • It reads a 2.5-second window and picks one of seven labels. Longer speech, changing emotion and labels outside the seven are not handled, and live microphone use has not been scored.
    • The dashboard is a local prototype, and the large trained weights are not in the public repository, so a real build would start from a fresh, tested package with its own accuracy check.

    FAQ

    Questions about emotion recognition

    What is speech emotion recognition?

    Speech emotion recognition is software that listens to a voice clip and guesses the emotion from tone, pitch and energy rather than the words. This project labels seven emotions: angry, disgust, fear, happy, neutral, sad and surprise. It reads numbers that describe the sound from the audio and passes them to a neural network.

    How accurate is speech emotion recognition?

    In this project, 93.87% on a random 20% test split of 9,730 samples. That figure does not cover new speakers, because altered copies of one clip can land on both sides of the split. Accuracy on unseen voices is likely to be lower, so a real project would first measure it by splitting speakers apart.

    Can it work with my own recordings?

    It can be retrained on your recordings if they are labelled with the emotions you care about. Quality depends on the microphone, the language and the number of speakers. I would first test it on a sample of your audio and report how well it holds.

    How much of the speech emotion model did you design yourself?

    The network design is not mine. The training recipe is a public Kaggle notebook, which I adapted and retrained on four datasets. My own work is the testing and its stated limits, the live microphone notebook, the Streamlit dashboard and the saved model files, and I say so plainly on this page.

    Why does the repository quote 97.25% when this page says 93.87%?

    The repository README and the notebook's file name quote 97.25%, but the notebook's own output never shows that figure. The saved model scores 93.87% on the 9,730-sample test split, so I report that number, with its caveats. The README figure is not supported by the notebook's output, so this page does not use it.

    Does it work in real time from a microphone?

    A notebook records 2.5 seconds at a time, extracts the same features, scales them and prints an emotion, then repeats until stopped. The dashboard can record 1.5 to 5 seconds and shows a bar for each emotion. Neither has been scored for accuracy on live speech, so treat live output as a demo.

    How long does it take to train a speech emotion model?

    Here, the 20 training passes took about ten hours in total, roughly half an hour each, and extracting features from all 12,162 clips took about two minutes spread over 20 processor cores. Time depends on the computer and the data, so I would size a new project on a sample first.
