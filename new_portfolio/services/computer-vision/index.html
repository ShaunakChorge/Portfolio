    Computer vision development services

    Vision projects succeed or fail on the quality of the data and the testing, not on model names. I start from your footage or images, agree how success is measured and report what the numbers show.

    I offer computer vision development services: software that finds, counts or sorts things in photos and video. Examples are crowd counting, spotting events in camera footage and matching faces. I measure how accurate it is on your own footage and report what it was tested on. I also deliver a small app or report your team can use.

    Who it is for

    Who this is for

    • Facility and safety managers

      People who must watch how busy a space gets. They need counts, an alert at a set limit and a saved frame as evidence. A team prototype I worked on counts people crossing a line in recorded video.

    • Product teams adding video checks

      Teams that want a first-pass screen for uploaded videos or images, such as flagging possible face-swap edits, with a clear report and an honest accuracy figure before they invest more.

    • Security and operations leads

      Teams with more cameras and photos than people to review them. A tool pre-sorts the material, so staff look at a short list of flagged items instead of everything.

    • Research and student teams

      Groups that need a documented, repeatable detection or classification workflow, with the data split and results written down so another person can rerun it and get the same numbers.

    Problems I solve

    Problems I solve

    • ProblemToo much footage for people to review

      How I approach itVideo analytics software samples frames, counts people or labels possible events, and saves the frame behind each result. A person then checks only the flagged moments. The team CCTV prototype works this way on recorded video.

    • ProblemAccuracy figures that do not hold up

      How I approach itIn project work I keep test footage apart from training footage, split by video, and state the split beside every figure. The team deepfake detector's 74.16% is labelled as a random frame-level split, because near-identical frames from one video can sit on both sides.

    • ProblemBusy, dark or blurry scenes confuse the model

      How I approach itSimple background removal can undercount when people overlap. I test on your hardest footage and report where the tool fails. For crowded scenes I can switch to a trained detector with a tracker (it follows each person from frame to frame).

    • ProblemReviewers cannot see why something was flagged

      How I approach itEach result keeps the evidence behind it. In the deepfake app, a chart scores every frame, a note explains each confidence band and a PDF report can be downloaded. The CCTV prototype saves its evidence frames in MongoDB.

    • ProblemAlerts do not reach the right person

      How I approach itAlerts go to a channel your staff already use. The CCTV prototype I worked on sent a WhatsApp message through Twilio when the crowd limit was reached, with a reference to the saved frame.

    Deliverables and fit

    What you receive

    Deliverables

    • A detection or classification tool for your images or video
    • Code that samples video frames and cleans them up first (OpenCV)
    • A short results note that states how the tool was tested
    • A small Streamlit or Flask app for uploading files and viewing results
    • Alerts or record-keeping, such as saving to a database or sending a WhatsApp message (Twilio)
    • A documented repository or notebook, so results can be reproduced

    What I need from you

    • Sample footage or images from the real setting, with permission to use them, covering the angles, lighting and times of day that matter.
    • A clear list of what must be detected or counted, and which mistake costs you more: a missed event or a false alarm.
    • Someone who knows your site and can mark the correct answers on a sample of footage, because accuracy is measured against those marks.
    • The setup: how many cameras, recorded clips or a live stream, the hardware available, and where alerts should be sent.

    The process

    How a computer vision project runs

    1. 01

      Scope

      We define what must be detected, in what conditions, and how accuracy will be judged.

    2. 02

      Baseline

      A first model, or a ready-made one, tried on a sample of your data.

    3. 03

      Evaluate

      Accuracy measured on data the model has not seen, with the data split stated.

    4. 04

      Package

      A small app or API, documentation and a repository others can rerun.

    Technology

    Technology, and where it fits

    OpenCV
    Reads video, samples frames, resizes and cleans images, and handles background removal, line-crossing counts and classic face matching.
    YOLOv8
    A fast detector that draws a box around each object. I have run the small YOLOv8n model on video frames.
    PyTorch
    Runs ready-made detection and classification models, including Faster R-CNN and the Vision Transformer used for crime labels.
    TensorFlow / Keras
    Trains small image networks on labelled frames, as in the college deepfake detector, and saves them for an app to load.
    Hugging Face Transformers
    Loads a ready-made Vision Transformer for frame labels, so a first baseline needs no training.
    Streamlit
    Builds the upload-and-review app, with a confidence chart and a downloadable PDF report made with ReportLab.
    Flask
    Serves simple pages where reviewers see the latest detections and saved evidence frames.
    MongoDB
    Stores evidence frames and crime labels, so alerts can be checked later.
    Twilio
    Sends WhatsApp alerts, such as a message when a crowd limit is crossed.

    Decide together

    Trade-offs we decide together

    • Ready-made model or custom training

      A ready-made detector is quick and often good enough for common objects such as people. Custom training pays off for unusual objects or camera views. I test a ready-made model on your footage first and train only if the numbers fall short.

    • Fewer false alarms or fewer misses

      A detector can be tuned to raise fewer false alarms or to miss fewer real events, but not both at once. We agree which mistake costs more for you, set the alert level to match and report both kinds of error.

    • Every frame or a frame every few seconds

      Checking one frame every few seconds is light on hardware and often enough to label events or count who is in view. Counting people as they cross a line needs every frame. The team CCTV prototype used both. I measure speed on your hardware before we choose.

    FAQ

    Questions about computer vision projects

    What is YOLO object detection?

    YOLO, short for You Only Look Once, is a fast method that finds and labels objects in an image or video frame. It works in one pass and draws a box around each. I have used the small YOLOv8n version on video frames. It suits live counting and alerts, though accuracy has to be tested on your own footage.

    How accurate is computer vision?

    Accuracy is measured on a sample of your own footage first, using data the model has not seen. The result is reported clearly, together with the way the data was split. The deepfake detector from my college team reports 74.16% on a random frame-level split, so every figure states what it covers.

    How much does a computer vision project cost?

    It depends on how much footage or how many images you have, how much labelling is needed, the accuracy you need and whether the tool must run live. I do not publish a price list. After a short brief I suggest a fixed price or hourly billing for a clear scope, and we agree it in writing before I start.

    Can you train a model on my own images or video?

    Yes, for small to medium sets of images. A college team I was part of trained a neural network on frames from a public deepfake dataset, and during an internship I built one that sorts plastic-waste images. I have also used ready-made models such as Faster R-CNN, YOLOv8n and a Vision Transformer. We first check your labelled data.

    Can it work on live camera feeds?

    The team CCTV prototype read recorded video and checked frames on a schedule, one every three seconds for the crime labels. Live feeds are a different job: real-time speed depends on camera quality, the number of cameras and your hardware. I would connect a test stream and measure that during planning rather than assume it.

    What about privacy for face or surveillance projects?

    Those projects need consent, clear rules on how long data is kept and legal review on your side. I raise privacy questions during planning and can build in options such as limited data retention and restricted access to stored images. The legal decisions stay with you.

    Can computer vision count people in a crowd?

    Yes, within limits. The CCTV prototype I worked on counted people crossing virtual lines using OpenCV background removal and raised an alert at a set limit. Dense or dark scenes are harder, so I would test on your own footage and, if needed, move to a trained detector and tracker for steadier counts.

    Does my video have to be uploaded to an online service?

    Not necessarily. Detection models such as YOLOv8 run on your own computer or server, so video can stay in your building. In the CCTV prototype I worked on, the README calls for a local MongoDB database for evidence frames, and the WhatsApp alert carried only a short text. The right setup depends on your privacy rules and hardware.
