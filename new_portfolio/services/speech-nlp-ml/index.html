    Who it is for

    Who this is for

    • Teams with voice recordings

      Teams that want to flag tone or mood in recorded calls or check-ins, such as calls worth a follow-up, and need to know how far the labels can be trusted.

    • Support and messaging teams

      Teams that receive many emails or texts and want unwanted messages filtered out automatically, with very few genuine messages blocked by mistake.

    • Analysts with spreadsheet data

      People who hold tables of past results and want to predict a number, such as a price or a cost, and see how much a stronger model adds over a simple one.

    • Founders testing an idea

      Product owners who need a quick proof of concept on their own labelled data, with a clear accuracy figure, before they fund a bigger build.

    Problems I solve

    Problems I solve

    • ProblemA model's accuracy figure cannot be trusted

      How I approach itFor client work I hold back a test set the model never sees, split by speaker or source where possible, and state the split. I report precision (how many flags were right) and recall (how many real cases were found), not accuracy alone.

    • ProblemIt is unclear whether a complex model is needed

      How I approach itI train a simple baseline first, then the stronger model, and report both. In my house-price model a Random Forest (many decision trees averaged) reached an R-squared of 0.89 (1.0 is perfect) against 0.80 for a simple linear baseline, both on held-out houses.

    • ProblemRaw audio and text are not ready for a model

      How I approach itAudio becomes numbers for loudness (energy), how often the signal crosses zero and MFCCs (a compact description of a voice's sound), extracted with librosa. Text is cleaned and scored by word frequency (TF-IDF). Tables get gaps filled and values scaled.

    • ProblemToo few labelled examples

      How I approach itBefore any training I count examples per category and report gaps early. Where audio is scarce, altering clips with noise and pitch changes adds variety: in the speech model I retrained, 12,162 clips became 48,648 samples.

    • ProblemA trained model sits unused in a notebook

      How I approach itI wrap it in a small Streamlit app where people upload, record or paste and see the result, and in a tested FastAPI service when your own software must call it.

    Deliverables and fit

    What you receive

    Deliverables

    • Data preparation and feature extraction (librosa for audio, word-frequency scoring for text, pandas for tables)
    • Model training, with a comparison against a simple baseline
    • A results report that states how the data was split
    • A Streamlit app: upload, record or paste, then see the result
    • A saved model and a script that uses it
    • A reproducible notebook and a README

    What I need from you

    • Labelled examples: recordings, messages or table rows, each marked with the correct answer by someone who knows the subject.
    • A plain statement of what the model should predict, which mistakes cost most and what accuracy would make it useful.
    • Details of how the data was collected: speakers, devices, sources and dates, and any person or customer who appears many times.
    • Whether the data holds personal information, where it may be stored, and where the finished model must run.

    Technology

    Technology, and where it fits

    librosa
    Loads audio and measures it, giving the model numbers for loudness, zero-crossing rate and MFCCs.
    TensorFlow / Keras
    Trains neural networks, such as the five-block network I retrained for speech emotion, and saves them for reuse.
    scikit-learn
    Provides TF-IDF word scoring, Naive Bayes, Random Forest and linear models, plus data splits and accuracy measures.
    NLTK
    Handles natural language processing (NLP) steps: splits text into words, drops common words and reduces each word to its stem.
    pandas
    Loads and cleans tables and datasets, and fills gaps such as missing values with the median.
    Streamlit
    Builds the small app where people upload, record or paste, then see the result with a chart.
    sounddevice
    Records short microphone clips, so a model can be tried live on a voice.
    Hugging Face Transformers
    Loads ready-made pretrained models to use as a first baseline before any training from scratch.
    FastAPI
    Serves a saved model as an API that your own software can call, with tests and a Docker setup.

    Decide together

    Trade-offs we decide together

    • Simple baseline or neural network

      A simple model such as Naive Bayes or a linear fit is quick to build and easy to explain. A neural network may add accuracy on audio or images. I build the simple one first and keep the stronger model only if the gap justifies the extra complexity.

    • Test on new speakers or random samples

      A random split is quick, but it can flatter the result when one speaker or source appears on both sides. Splitting by speaker, customer or source gives a lower, fairer number. On client data I split that way where possible; my speech project used a random split.

    • Fewer wrong blocks or fewer misses

      A filter can be tuned to avoid blocking genuine messages or to catch more spam, but not both at once. On its 1,034 test messages my spam filter flagged no genuine message and missed 30 spam messages. We choose by what each mistake costs you.

    FAQ

    Questions about machine learning projects

    How do you deploy a machine learning model?

    A trained model is wrapped in a small FastAPI service with tests and a Docker setup, the same way I build other backends. Your apps can then send data to it and read the result. Handover includes instructions for running it, so your team can move it to a server or cloud machine later.

    How much data do I need to train a model?

    It depends on the task. Simple tasks with clear categories may need a few hundred labelled examples per category, while audio or images usually need thousands. My spam filter used 5,169 messages after duplicates were removed, and my speech model 12,162 clips. I count your examples first and tell you early if there are too few.

    Can you classify my own audio or text data?

    Yes, if you have labelled examples and a clear target. I have built a neural network on audio features. I have also built a spam classifier on text (word-frequency scoring with a Naive Bayes classifier). Each has a small app around it. Before training, we check that you have enough examples of each category.

    How do you report accuracy?

    Every figure comes with the data split it was measured on. The speech emotion model I retrained reaches 93.87% on a random 20% test split. Because that split can put altered copies of one clip on both sides, it says little about new voices. For your recordings I test on speakers the model has not heard.

    Do you use ready-made models?

    When they fit. Pretrained Hugging Face models are a sensible start for many text and audio tasks. My speech model began as a public training recipe that I retrained and tested on four datasets. I compare against a simple baseline, so you see what extra complexity buys, and I say when the simple option is enough.

    Can you build a prediction model from my spreadsheet data?

    Yes, if you have past rows with the outcome you want to predict, such as a price or cost. I clean the table, fill gaps, compare a simple linear baseline with a stronger model such as a Random Forest, and report the typical error in your own units. A small app lets your team try it.

    How do you check a model has not just memorised its data?

    I keep a test set the model never trains on and compare its score there with its training score. A large gap means it has memorised. The speech network I retrained also uses dropout (switching off random parts during training) to limit this. For client audio, altered copies of a clip stay on one side of the split.

    Can you check the accuracy of a model someone else built?

    Yes. I need the saved model or code, the data it used and the figure that was claimed. I rerun it, check how the data was split, compare it with a simple baseline and report what I measure. The same habit exposed a flattering 93% score on a bank text-to-SQL assistant, where running the queries showed far less.
